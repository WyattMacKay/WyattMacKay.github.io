Assignment 1

Inspiration

University of New Brunswick
MAAC 3103 • Media Design I

I consider inspiration to be anything that gets my brain rolling. There is no criteria of what can be inspiring, as inspiration can be pulled from anywhere. I often find myself easily inspired by the world around me and most media I engage with. I seldom walk away from a movie or game simply thinking “that was fun”; instead, I tend to think of all the deliberate choices made in production, and what I would do differently. These choices could be anything from storytelling devices to entire character arcs.

For this assignment, I chose three creators from across Canada to write about. These three are brand new to me, and I was honestly surprised how much their works impacted and inspired me.

Photographer

James Brady was a Métis politician, former war artist, and social rights activist in the Prairies. Brady was active from the 1930’s until his eventual disappearance in 1967.

Brady’s snapshot photography is very emotionally provocative. His deliberate spontaneous style has me empathize with each of his photo’s subject. These photos do not feel like I am being told a story, but instead shown someone’s life in a way that feels like a call to action.

I draw strong comparisons between Brady’s photographs and the photographs of 19th century journalist Jacob Riis, especially those depicted in his book How the Other Half Lives. Neither of these men were photographers by trade, but instead used photography to amplify their work and activism. Both men used snapshots to appeal to the humanity of others by showing the daily lives of an oppressed and exploited group; Brady primarily depicted the Indigenous peoples of Canada, and Riis, the working class.

In the modern day, one might rarely feel sonder. Brady’s work reminds me that everyone has a unique life, but despite that uniqueness, we all share similarities.

Videographer

Sarah Polley is an award-winning filmmaker from Toronto. Polley is an Officer of the Order of Canada and won an Academy Award in 2023 for her film adaptation of Women Talking.

My experience with Polley’s work is only with her film Women Talking. The film follows the women of a Mennonite community after discovering that they have been getting drugged and raped for decades by catching a man in the act. The perpetrators are taken by the city police for their own protection, but the remaining men in the community – bar a minute-taker, August – leave the settlement to post the rapists’ bail. While the men are away, eleven women must meet and come to the decision on behalf of the rest, of whether to stay and fight the men, or to leave the community and risk damnation.

Women Talking is reminiscent of the 1957 film, 12 Angry Men. The two films consist of a room of twelve individuals forced to unanimously agree on a crucial decision. Both films are excellent at depicting tension and emotion through composition. Despite what the name may suggest, Women Talking does much more than tell a story. Polley manages to captivate me like few others; her scenes without dialogue motivate me to try wordless communication in my own work.

Painter

Alex Colville was a Maritime painter and former war artist. Colville studied and taught Fine Arts at Mount Allison University. Colville is credited for leading the Maritime Realist art movement.

As someone who was born and raised in New Brunswick, I find Colville’s work has me feeling nostalgic for the present. His art depicts not only the look, but the feel of Prince Edward Island and northern New Brunswick. I imagine his art more accurately describes Nova Scotia, which gives me wanderlust for the province.

During World War II, Colville photographed the Bergen-Belsen concentration camp, which he used as direct – and I suspect often indirect – inspiration for his paintings. Colville’s paintings radiate powerful feelings of isolation and vulnerability; his tasteful use of light and darkness serves this purpose greatly. Colville’s drab art gives me a strong feeling of lonesome, regardless of how coupled his subjects are.

What draws me to Colville’s art more than anything is the evolution of his level of facial detail: In the 50’s, Colville painted very detailed human faces; from the late 60’s to the 80’s he tended to mostly obstruct faces; from the 90’s until his death, Colville’s subjects’ faces become less detailed and often blurry or smeared. This change of style just rouses countless ideas from my head.

There is no shortage of talent here in Canada. The three creators I chose for inspiration are all incredibly talented in their own styles and mediums. James Brady uses snapshot photography as a tool to educate. Sarah Polley uses film to show and tell the stories that many real women share. Alex Colville uses oil painting to give the viewer a sense of longing and vulnerability. Interestingly, all three not only are from Canada, but also have travelled to Europe; Brady and Colville as war artists in the Second World War, and Polley as a film star and maker.

The works created by these artists also have commonalities, and I believe these commonalities are what draws me to them so fiercely:

  • Brady and Polley both depict socially disadvantaged people in their works, and I always find myself rooting for underdogs in fiction and reality.
  • Brady and Colville excel at depicting life the way it feels; I find art typically more impactful when I can relate to it.
  • Polley and Colville both share an excellent sense of showing silent emotions through composition, lighting, and expression.
  • All three of them gravitate to bleak and dreary works of which I find myself insatiably devouring. There is such a gritty feeling to each of these pieces that has me subconsciously introspecting on why it makes me feel the indescribable way I feel when I see them. My favourite aspect of drab works, is how powerful the contrast is with lively comparisons.

While I do draw comparisons from my prior inspirations to each creator, I find both Brady and especially Colville’s works remind me strongly of Marty Robbins’ music. I have a weakness for subtle cowboy themes that are present in both artists’ work. They all share a particular sense of lonesome and secludity that is hard to match, but it gives me an overwhelming urge to try myself.
